**Rubber hose animation** was the first
[animation](animation "wikilink") style that became standardized in the
[American animation](American_animation "wikilink") industry. The
defining feature of the style is "rubber-hose limbs"—arms, and sometimes
legs, that are typically drawn as flowing curves, without
[articulation](joint "wikilink") (no hinged knees or elbows).[^1] This
style makes most moving animated objects exhibit a curving motion,
resembling the physical properties of a rubber
[hose](hose "wikilink").[^2] The most famous examples of this style are
[Oswald the Lucky Rabbit](Oswald_the_Lucky_Rabbit "wikilink"), [Mickey
Mouse](Mickey_Mouse "wikilink"), [Popeye](Popeye "wikilink") and [Felix
the Cat](Felix_the_Cat "wikilink"). While the rubber hose art style fell
out of fashion by the late 1930s, it has experienced a renewed interest
since the early 2010s.

## History

### Beginnings and rise

[thumb\|thumbtime=358\|The 1930 [Fleischer
Studios](Fleischer_Studios "wikilink") short *[Swing You
Sinners!](Swing_You_Sinners! "wikilink")*, an example of Fleischer's
signature mix of rubber hose animation, surreal visuals and dark subject
matter.](File:Swing_You_Sinners!_Original_Print.webm "wikilink") In the
early days of hand drawn animation in the 1920s, American animation
production companies were not located in
[Hollywood](Hollywood,_Los_Angeles,_California "wikilink") but in [New
York City](New_York_City "wikilink"). Back then, animation was a new
phenomenon and there were no experienced
[animators](animators "wikilink") around, yet there were skilled artists
employed at newspapers creating [comic strips](comic_strip "wikilink"),
another relatively new medium.[^3] Many of them became fascinated with
the introduction of moving drawings and put their skills into this new
format.

As animated cartoons became popular, the industry's small workforce of
animators struggled to keep up.[^4] Their design choices were a mix of
intention and necessity.[^5]

Studios had to be sensitive to any new business trend to survive the
competition. A consequence of this was that the designs of the most
successful and popular cartoons had a great impact on the rest of the
animation business. One such example was [Otto
Messmer](Otto_Messmer "wikilink")'s *[Felix the
Cat](Felix_the_Cat "wikilink")*, who quickly spawned imitators from
different studios.[^6] [Bill Nolan](Bill_Nolan_(animator) "wikilink") is
credited with the introduction of the rubber-hose style while animating
Felix the Cat shorts in the 1920s.[^7] Nolan altered the design of the
title character, giving Felix a rounder, smoother look. This new design,
besides being faster to draft, also gave characters the "rubbery" limbs,
with no knees or elbow joints, which they could stretch to far distances
or be neatly tied in knots.[^8][^9] Using such fluid, flexible movements
made the animation process easier and faster, and the surreal quality of
the resulting pictures captivated the audiences. Nolan would introduce a
more refined style to [Universal
Pictures](Universal_Pictures "wikilink")' [Oswald the Lucky
Rabbit](Oswald_the_Lucky_Rabbit "wikilink") series after he found work
at Universal's newly founded [cartoon
division](Walter_Lantz_Productions "wikilink"); the series' earlier
animation style by [Walt Disney](Walt_Disney "wikilink") and [Ub
Iwerks](Ub_Iwerks "wikilink"), a less surreal variant which is more
reliant on mechanical humor, would form the basis for the popular
[*Mickey Mouse*](Mickey_Mouse_(film_series) "wikilink") series.[^10]

### Introduction of sound

The rubber hose styled film found popularity with the use of its
sound.[^11] While [Disney](The_Walt_Disney_Company "wikilink") found
wild success for the use of music in their *Mickey Mouse* films, this
studio was not the first or only to use sound in their film. Following
just second to Disney, The Fleischer Brothers (who would eventually help
found Red Seal distribution company, later renamed [Fleischer
Studios](Fleischer_Studios "wikilink")) also used musical elements to
enhance their films.[^12] They developed *Song Car-Tunes*, a project
that invited the audience to sing along to a tune throughout the film.
The bouncing ball that is used in sing-alongs today to bounce
word-to-word was introduced by the Fleischer brothers as well. Many
other creators understood that the frame-by-frame style lent itself well
to the addition of music due to the frames being able to be synced
perfectly with the corresponding sound. Disney, wanting to press this
idea even further, developed the *[Silly
Symphonies](Silly_Symphony "wikilink")* series.[^13] Moving away from
slapstick humor, this series focused more on the synchronization of the
sound. This meant that the shorts were tamer with the music being the
focal point as opposed to visual gags.

### Decline

Rubber-hose animation gradually faded away as cartoons were made more
sophisticated. The fad of [realism](Realism_(arts) "wikilink") in
animation, with the appearance of lifelike [anatomy](anatomy "wikilink")
and natural movement, added to more advanced film techniques like
[Technicolor](Technicolor "wikilink"), led to the decline of the
rubber-hose style.

[Walt Disney](Walt_Disney "wikilink")'s massive success with his first
full-length animated feature film *[Snow White and the Seven
Dwarfs](Snow_White_and_the_Seven_Dwarfs_(1937_film) "wikilink")*,
released in 1937, was key in changing the trend.[^14][^15] In Hollywood,
most cartoon studios of the early 1930s (many of them founded by former
Disney animators) copied this new realistic trend. In New York, Disney's
influence took longer to grab hold. [Fleischer
Studios](Fleischer_Studios "wikilink") held on to the rubber-hose style
the longest, finally conforming to the more contemporary West Coast
animation style by 1940.

## Influence

The rubber-hose style eventually appeared in some later cartoons, such
as [The Warner Siblings](Yakko,_Wakko_and_Dot "wikilink") from
*[Animaniacs](Animaniacs "wikilink")* by [Warner Bros.
Animation](Warner_Bros._Animation "wikilink"). It has also been a major
influence in video games such as *[Cuphead](Cuphead "wikilink")* (and
its [animated series adaptation](The_Cuphead_Show! "wikilink")) and
*[Mouse: P.I. for Hire](Mouse:_P.I._for_Hire "wikilink")*.[^16][^17]

### Theatrical film

In 2013, [Walt Disney Animation
Studios](Walt_Disney_Animation_Studios "wikilink") produced *[Get a
Horse!](Get_a_Horse! "wikilink")*, a 3D animated slapstick comedy short
film using the style.[^18] It combines black-and-white hand-drawn
animation with color CGI.[^19] The short features characters from late
1920s Disney cartoons and archival recordings of Walt Disney in a
posthumous role as Mickey Mouse.[^20][^21] It is the first original
Mickey Mouse theatrical animated short since *[Runaway
Brain](Runaway_Brain "wikilink")* (1995) and the first appearance of
Oswald the Lucky Rabbit in a Disney animated production in 85 years.

### Television

The 2013 *[Mickey Mouse](Mickey_Mouse_(TV_series) "wikilink")* series
uses an animation style reminiscent of rubber hose animation through
[Toon Boom](Toon_Boom_Animation "wikilink") and [Flash
animation](Flash_animation "wikilink"), complementing the art style and
humor of the original series.[^22]
